What moves Beijing's PM2.5, hour by hour

A model fed only the weather — the airport's METARs, ERA5 reanalysis, and the calendar — explains three-quarters of the hourly variance in the city's PM2.5 two years past the end of its training data. Here is what it learned, and what it says about wind, rain and season once everything else is held still.

The model

The target is hourly city-mean PM2.5 (the site's own CNEMC series, 33 stations), modelled as log1p because a distribution with a median of 31 and a maximum of 641 needs it. Inputs are the ZBAA METAR (temperature, dewpoint, humidity, wind vector, gusts, pressure, present-weather codes for rain, snow and dust), ERA5 reanalysis for central Beijing (real precipitation amounts, boundary-layer height, 100 m wind, cloud, radiation, soil moisture), rolling 3–72 h means and sums of each, and the calendar as sine/cosine pairs for hour, day of year and weekday, plus a Chinese New Year window.

Training runs Dec 2013 – 2022, 2023 is held for early stopping, and 2024 – Sep 2026 is never touched until scoring. That split is the hard version of the problem, because 2024's Beijing (annual mean 31 µg/m³) is not 2016's (74). The year itself is not an input: neural nets extrapolated the 2013–22 decline as a straight line and predicted 2025 cleaner than it was. Instead each hour sees the city's mean PM2.5 over the trailing year, lagged a month — an emissions baseline that carries no weather.

Three things were deliberately kept out because they are what PM2.5 looks like, not what causes it: visibility, the haze/mist/smoke codes, and — found the hard way — whether the METAR carried a cloud group at all, since a report is CAVOK only when visibility is at least 10 km.

Gradient boosting and the recurrent net land in the same place, which suggests the ceiling is in the inputs rather than the model class. The GRU is handed raw hourly weather for the past 120 hours and no hand-built rolling features at all; it rediscovers the same lag structure. Persistence (last hour's value) scores R² 0.98 at one hour ahead and −0.16 at 24 h, so the weather model is a different kind of object: it knows nothing about yesterday's air.

Wind is most of it

Wind history carries 54% of the model's gain and the single most important input is the northerly component of the 100 m wind averaged over the past 24 hours — not the wind now, and not its speed. Speed alone is the wrong variable. A steady 24 h wind at 6 m/s leaves 13 µg/m³ if it comes from the north-west and 57 if it comes from the south, and a stronger southerly is worse: at 9 m/s the model reads 68, because that wind is importing Hebei's air up the plain.

The 24 hours after the north wind starts

Taking every onset of a strong north wind (≥ 5 m/s from within 45° of north, after 12 quiet hours) and averaging the measured PM2.5 around it: the air clears within six hours, bottoms out at +6 to +12 h at roughly a third of the pre-frontal level, and takes two full days to climb back. The shape is the same in every season; only the height differs.

Spring is where your instinct about sandstorms holds. In the 29 onsets where the METAR reported dust in the following day, PM2.5 rose from 69 to 79 six hours in, before falling. Without dust — 339 onsets — spring behaves like every other season.

Rain, controlling for everything else

This is the question the model was built for, and the answer has two halves that pull in opposite directions.

Washout is real and scales with amount. Take every hour with rain in the past 24 h, ask the model to predict it, then zero every precipitation input and predict again with wind, humidity, boundary layer, season and hour all left exactly as they were. Rain alone lowers the prediction by 17% for a trace-to-2 mm day, 24% at 2–5 mm, 46% at 20–50 mm and 60% above 50 mm. A model-free check agrees: comparing wet and dry hours inside the same month × wind sector × wind speed × time-of-day × era cell gives −25% across 1,065 cells.

But rain arrives wet. Relative humidity is the model's strongest non-wind lever — it moves the prediction from 31 µg/m³ at 10% RH to 60 at 100% — because hygroscopic particles swell and a saturated stagnant airmass is the recipe for haze. Compare a rainy day not with "the same day without rain" but with a genuinely dry day at that month's typical humidity, and light rain is net-neutral to slightly positive; the day comes out cleaner only past about 20 mm.

Winter flips the sign. The model-free comparison by month shows precipitation hours running 23–45% cleaner than dry hours from March to November, then +19% in December and +84% in January. Winter precipitation in Beijing is snow or drizzle out of a moist southerly airmass under a low boundary layer: the exact conditions that build a haze episode. The rain is still washing — the model's washout-only estimate for winter is −26% — but the weather it comes with is worth more than that, so a snowy day is a bad-air day.

The effect lingers. Removing a six-hour block of rain from the model's inputs changes its prediction by 28–30% while the rain is falling or just finished, 24% a day later and still 18% two days later. Beijing's air takes about as long to re-load after rain as after a north wind.

The rest of the weather

Partial dependence: each panel varies one input (and its rolling copies) across its range for the same 20,000 sampled hours, holding everything else fixed, and reports the mean prediction.

  • Season is the shape you know — but note this is the seasonal effect after wind, humidity and boundary layer are accounted for. March still peaks; what remains there is dust and heating-season carry-over the weather inputs don't name.
  • Boundary layer height: below 100 m the model reads 56 µg/m³, above 800 m about 35, and it saturates — once the lid is off, more height adds nothing.
  • Wind speed on its own is non-monotonic: calm is bad (52), 4 m/s is the clean point (30), and 8–10 m/s is worse again (40) because at those speeds the model is seeing strong southerlies and dust.
  • Hour of day is almost flat once the boundary layer is an input; the familiar night-time hump is the boundary layer collapsing, not the clock. Weekday is exactly flat — there is no weekend effect in Beijing's PM2.5, in any year.

Weather did not clean the air

Hold the emissions baseline at 2014's and give the model each subsequent year's actual weather. It predicts 78–87 µg/m³ every year: the 2013–25 decline from 87 to 27 is essentially all emissions, with the weather of 2017 and 2025 somewhat kinder and 2023–24 somewhat harsher than average. This is a coarse counterfactual — the model has only seen each weather regime paired with its own era — but the gap is too large to be a modelling artefact.

More data: what's overhead, and what's upwind

Two more keyless sources were added after the first pass. Beijing's radiosonde (station 54511, from NOAA's IGRA archive) goes up twice a day and reports what the surface can't see: whether there is a lid, and which way the air is moving above the rooftops. The northerly wind at 925 hPa — about 750 m up — turns out to be the single strongest correlate of PM2.5 in the whole dataset (r = −0.51, against −0.36 for the airport's surface wind), and the 850 hPa-minus-surface temperature difference, the inversion, is the strongest non-wind one (+0.38). Adding the soundings takes the forward-test score from 0.748 to 0.762.

The second is the rest of the CNEMC network: hourly PM2.5 and PM10 for twenty cities around Beijing, grouped by direction. The south group (Baoding, Shijiazhuang, Langfang…) is the Hebei plain the south wind blows in from; the north-west group (Zhangjiakou, Hohhot, Xilingol…) is the steppe the dust comes from. Their current readings lift the score to 0.875 and cut the error to 6.4 µg/m³ — but the reading from thirty kilometres away is partly the same air, so that number is a nowcast, not an explanation. Lag the upwind readings and the benefit decays: half of it is gone after 12 hours, all of it after about two days.

Forecasting the week ahead

The forecast has two parts. A weather model trained only on inputs a forecast can supply — ERA5-style surface fields, boundary-layer height, and the sounding features re-derived from 925/850/700 hPa forecast levels — scores 0.711 on the test years when driven by reanalysis; that is the ceiling. A residual model then learns how the weather model's error at issue time persists: it takes the lead time, Beijing's own latest reading relative to what the weather alone would give, the upwind cities' latest readings, and what the wind and rain do between now and then. Each training row draws a random lead so one model covers the whole horizon and learns its own decay — persistence at one hour, weather-only by two days.

The honest test uses Open-Meteo's archive of what the forecast actually said one to seven days earlier, over 2024–26. Running the same model on the real forecast and on reanalysis separates the two sources of error: whatever the reanalysis-driven run gets wrong is ours, and the gap to the forecast-driven run belongs to the weather forecast.

This week's outlook

Where it still comes up short

  • Summer scores R² 0.58 against 0.86 in winter. At 20 µg/m³ the residual is secondary aerosol chemistry — photochemistry, ozone, regional sulfate — which no surface-weather input describes.
  • Spring dust is still the largest source of big misses. North-west PM10 now gives a day's warning when the plume is already over Inner Mongolia; a satellite dust product would give two.
  • Beyond two days the limit is the weather forecast, not this model. An ensemble forecast (several members rather than one deterministic run) would at least put honest error bars on days 3–7.
  • The worst hours are under-predicted (mean 80 vs 102 above 75 µg/m³). A quantile or Tweedie objective, or a classification head for episode days, would trade a little average skill for better peaks.
