The week ahead, from the weather

A model trained on twelve years of the city's own PM2.5 readings against the weather that produced them, run in your browser on the current forecast. Below it: how good the forecast is, and what the model learned about wind, rain and season along the way.

The forecast run is Open-Meteo's best-match model for central Beijing; the readings are the CNEMC city mean via its mirror; the upwind cities are the same publication. Everything is fetched fresh, so the page is never more than about ten minutes behind the network.

How good is it

The model has two parts. A weather model maps the hour's weather and its previous 72 hours — wind at the surface, at 100 m and at 925/850/700 hPa, boundary-layer height, humidity, rain, the inversion, the season and the city's emissions baseline over the trailing year — to PM2.5. A residual model then learns how far the city's latest reading sits from what the weather alone would give, and how quickly that gap decays with lead time, with the upwind cities' latest readings as a hint of what is coming.

It was fitted on December 2013 – 2022 and judged on 2024 – September 2026, years it never saw and whose air was half as dirty as the years it learned from. The honest test uses the weather forecasts that were actually issued one to seven days earlier over that period: the same model run on reanalysis instead — a perfect forecast — separates its own error from the weather forecast's.

Gradient boosting and a recurrent net land in the same place, so the ceiling is the inputs, not the model. Upwind readings are worth a great deal for six hours and nothing after two days, which is why the forecast leans on them early and on the weather later.

Wind is most of it

Wind history carries half of the model's explanatory power, and its single most important input is the northerly component of the 100 m wind averaged over the past 24 hours — not the wind now, and not its speed. A steady wind at 6 m/s leaves 13 µg/m³ if it comes from the north-west and 57 if it comes from the south, and a stronger southerly is worse: it is importing the Hebei plain's air. Above the rooftops the signal is cleaner still: the northerly wind at 925 hPa, from the radiosonde, is the strongest single correlate of PM2.5 in the whole dataset.

Rain, holding everything else still

Washout is real and scales with amount. Take every hour with rain in the past 24 h, ask the model to predict it, then zero every precipitation input with wind, humidity, boundary layer and season left exactly as they were: the prediction drops 17% for a trace-to-2 mm day, 24% at 2–5 mm, 46% at 20–50 mm, 60% above 50 mm. A model-free comparison of wet and dry hours in the same month, wind sector, wind speed and time of day agrees: −25%.

But rain arrives wet. Humidity is the strongest non-wind lever the model has — 31 µg/m³ at 10% RH, 60 at 100% — because particles swell in humid air and a saturated, stagnant airmass is the recipe for haze. Compared with a genuinely dry day at that month's typical humidity, light rain is net-neutral; the day comes out cleaner only past about 20 mm. In winter it flips outright: snowy and drizzly days are stagnant, humid, southerly days, so January's wet hours run far dirtier than its dry ones.

The rest of the weather

Each panel varies one input across its range for the same 20,000 sampled hours, holding everything else fixed. Season keeps its shape even after wind, humidity and boundary layer are accounted for. Below a 100 m boundary layer the model reads 56 µg/m³, above 800 m about 35, and then it saturates. Wind speed on its own is non-monotonic — calm is bad, 4 m/s is the clean point, and 8–10 m/s is worse again because at those speeds the model is seeing strong southerlies and dust. Hour of day goes nearly flat once the boundary layer is an input; weekday is exactly flat — Beijing has no weekend effect.

Weather did not clean the air

Hold the emissions baseline at 2014's and give the model each later year's actual weather: it predicts 78–87 µg/m³ every year. The fall from 87 to 27 is emissions, with the weather of 2017 and 2025 somewhat kinder and 2023–24 somewhat harsher than average.

How it is built

and what was deliberately left out

Target: hourly city-mean PM2.5 as log1p. Inputs: Capital Airport METARs, ERA5 reanalysis for central Beijing, the Nanjiao radiosonde, twenty surrounding cities' PM2.5 and PM10, and the calendar as sine/cosine pairs. Gradient-boosted trees; the year is not an input — networks extrapolated the 2013–22 decline as a line — the trailing-year mean stands in for it.

Left out on purpose because they are what PM2.5 looks like, not what causes it: visibility, the haze and mist codes, and whether the METAR carried a cloud group at all (a report is CAVOK only when visibility is 10 km or more — it was the third most important feature until it was caught).
